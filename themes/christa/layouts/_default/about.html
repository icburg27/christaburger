Professional Summary

I build governance that holds when the stakes are real.

I am a cybersecurity executive — currently CISO and Vice President of Cybersecurity — with twenty years in the discipline of deciding how organizations handle data, risk, identity, and consequence when the outcome actually matters. My work is the architecture of accountability: who decides, what values guide the decision, what risk is acceptable, how accountability flows, and how the system changes when reality changes.

I have spent my career on the structural side of security rather than the reactive side. Anyone can write a policy. The harder work — the work I do — is building decision systems that function under pressure, that an examiner can follow, that a board can act on, and that don't collapse the first time an assumption breaks.

Risk oversight and effective challenge

Governance only means something when someone independent can say no and make it stick. I build and operate the structures that make challenge possible: risk appetite expressed in terms an operator can act on, key risk indicators that move before the incident rather than after it, continuous monitoring that surfaces emerging exposure early, and escalation paths people actually use because they're clear enough to trust.

Compliance is a floor, not a ceiling. Trust is not a certificate on a wall — it is a signal system that customers, auditors, examiners, and executives can each read correctly, and it has to be engineered.

Regulated environments

My career includes financial services and trust — Charles Schwab and Pensco Trust Company — where the regulatory floor is not a matter of interpretation and the consequences of getting controls wrong are measured in client assets and consent orders. That grounding shapes how I work everywhere since: examiner-ready evidence, controls designed to be tested rather than described, and the discipline of writing things down before someone asks.

My MBA is in Finance, which means I can hold the risk conversation and the business conversation in the same sentence — capital, exposure, and cost of control, not just threat and vulnerability. Security leaders who can only speak in one of those registers get overruled in rooms where the other one is spoken.

Communicating risk to boards

I have served on a board of directors for fourteen years, which taught me the thing most security leaders learn too late: a board does not want the technical truth diluted — it wants the technical truth translated, with the reasoning available the moment someone asks for it. Residual risk in thirty seconds. The full model on request.

I write and speak for that audience regularly, including keynotes on governance, AI risk, and accountable operating models for boards, executive teams, and security and risk organizations.

Emerging technology risk

The governance question for AI is not "what can it do?" but "who is it, what is it for, where are its limits, and how do we know when it has drifted?" I work at that frontier — identity, intent architecture, drift detection, limits, and succession for systems that take real action — and I publish the frameworks openly, because the organizations facing this problem need working models faster than the standards bodies can produce them.

That work is the through-line of my writing: several dozen essays on governance, AI risk, and the operating models that survive contact with reality, along with The Automated Household, a field guide to designing systems that carry repetition so people can carry judgment.

How I lead

I build teams that can think, not just execute — because a risk function staffed by people who only follow procedure will miss exactly the thing procedures don't cover. I hire for judgment, develop for range, and give people the context to make good calls without me in the room.

The through-line of everything, from the boardroom to the frameworks I publish: governance is not bureaucracy. Governance is clarity. It's how an organization stops relying on the memory, goodwill, and heroics of individual people — and starts relying on something built.
